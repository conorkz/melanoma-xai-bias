# Repair the inference before targeting imaging informatics

**Submit to the Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine (JIIM) after two focused days of statistical repair, with BMC Medical Imaging as the fallback.** JIIM is the best match in the shortlist: its scope explicitly lists AI safety, fairness and bias, its evidence bar fits a retrospective multi-architecture audit, and its hybrid model lets a budget-limited author publish for **$0** under the subscription route; its abstract limit is **250 words** and its public guidelines state no manuscript word limit ([JIIM scope](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278/aims-and-scope), [JIIM journal page](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278), [JIIM guidelines](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278/submission-guidelines)). The paper is **not submission-ready today**. The reported 95% intervals average per-run bootstrap bounds, the AUC stability claim has no supporting test and the ViT's gain narrows the gap to non-significance, the external ViT improvement is seed-dependent, and the Grad-CAM analysis explains the predicted class rather than the missed melanoma. The core findings survive the repair: the CNN external means are negative, the EfficientNet-B0 versus ViT interaction is negative in every seed, and the false-negative lightness association is the robust fairness result. Fix the inference first; DDI and PASSION would add months and confound skin tone with imaging modality without addressing the statistical problems.

## JIIM is the best fit, and the subscription route makes it free

The shortlist narrows on three facts. The paper is a retrospective audit, not a prospective clinical study, so the clinical dermatology titles are the wrong evidence stage. Its contribution is fairness, calibration and explanation, not a new architecture, so a methods journal that demands algorithmic novelty is a poor first target. The author has a limited budget, so a hybrid journal's subscription route is worth more than a marginally higher impact factor. JIIM satisfies all three: it is the official journal of the Society for Imaging Informatics in Medicine (SIIM), its scope explicitly includes AI, computer-aided diagnosis, and AI safety, fairness and bias, it runs an open Dermatology collection, and it has published skin-cancer transformer work ([JIIM scope](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278/aims-and-scope), [JIIM Swin paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10278-024-01140-8)). The one scope risk is SIIM's stated preference for validation and implementation papers over stand-alone algorithm descriptions ([SIIM submission page](https://siim.org/resources/publications/submit-a-manuscript)). Frame the paper as a validation and audit of an imaging pipeline, with calibration monitoring and subgroup reporting as the implementation lesson, not as another HAM10000 benchmark.

The best realistic tier is JIIM, BMC Medical Imaging, and Diagnostics. JIIM is first because the scope match is the closest and the subscription route costs nothing; its **$4,890** open-access fee is the highest in the shortlist, so the recommendation flips to Diagnostics if open access is mandatory and unfunded ([JIIM APC](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278/how-to-publish-with-us)). BMC Medical Imaging is the safest acceptance in the tier: its stated criterion is scientific validity rather than perceived impact, it publishes HAM10000-style CNN benchmarks, and its median first decision is **22 days** ([BMC scope](https://bmcmedimaging.biomedcentral.com/submission-guidelines/aims-and-scope), [BMC journal page](https://link.springer.com/journal/12880)). It costs **$3,290**, which is the price of that safety ([BMC APC](https://link.springer.com/journal/12880/submission-guidelines)). Diagnostics is the speed and budget alternative: **CHF 2,600** (about $3,250), a **20.4-day** median first decision, **2.9 days** from acceptance to publication, and a 2026 reliability-audited XAI skin-lesion precedent ([Diagnostics](https://www.mdpi.com/journal/diagnostics), [Diagnostics APC](https://www.mdpi.com/journal/diagnostics/apc), [Diagnostics XAI](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics16203368)). Cancers (CHF 2,900) and Bioengineering (CHF 2,700) are same-family alternatives; neither changes the tier. Before budgeting any APC, check the institution's read-and-publish agreements with Springer Nature and Elsevier; an institutional deal can cover the JIIM open-access fee or the BMC APC and change the cost ranking.

The stretch tier is Computer Methods and Programs in Biomedicine (CMPB), Computers in Biology and Medicine (CIBM), Scientific Reports, and PLOS Digital Health. CMPB has the closest methodological precedent, a 2024 study of skin colour bias in skin lesion segmentation, and a free subscription route ([CMPB fairness paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2024.108044)). It also sets a **3,500-word** target and a **50-reference** limit, which would require cutting the current 6,750-word manuscript nearly in half; it is a target for a shortened revision, not the first submission ([CMPB guide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/computer-methods-and-programs-in-biomedicine/publish/guide-for-authors)). CIBM has no word cap and a free subscription route, but its published acceptance rate is **12%** and it rejects minor architectural changes without strong baselines ([CIBM insights](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/computers-in-biology-and-medicine/about/insights)). Scientific Reports has a sound-science bar and a fairness precedent, but it caps the main text at **4,500 words** and the abstract at **200 words**, charges **$2,850**, and takes about **4.3 months** of total handling on author reports ([Scientific Reports guidelines](https://www.nature.com/srep/author-instructions/submission-guidelines), [SciRev](https://scirev.org/journal/scientific-reports/)). PLOS Digital Health fits the digital-health and fairness framing, but it expects implementation and generalizability, charges **$3,165**, and its Web of Science status is ESCI in some trackers and SCIE in others; verify before submitting ([PLOS Digital Health guidelines](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/s/submission-guidelines), [PLOS fees](https://plos.org/publish/fees/)).

The avoid list is specific. JAMA Dermatology, the British Journal of Dermatology, and JEADV are the wrong evidence stage: their recent melanoma AI work is prospective, multicenter, or evidence-synthesis based, and a single-dataset HAM10000 audit will not clear desk review ([JAMA Dermatology review](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/fullarticle/2846489), [BJD trial](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjd/ljae021), [JEADV BCC study](https://doi.org/10.1111/jdv.70099)). npj Digital Medicine expects generalizability and implementation, and a single-dataset audit is below its bar. Medical Image Analysis (MIA) wants a new method, and its median time to acceptance is **253 days**; a benchmark audit without a new algorithm is a poor use of both the submission and the wait ([MIA insights](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/medical-image-analysis/about/insights)). IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics (JBHI) gives regular papers **8 free pages** and charges **$250 to $350 per page** beyond that, which is a bad match for 11 tables and 6 figures ([JBHI policy](https://www.embs.org/jbhi/editorial-policy)). Artificial Intelligence in Medicine has a **303-day** median to acceptance and a 4.9-month first review on SciRev, and it states that mere application of well-known algorithms is not original ([AIM insights](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/artificial-intelligence-in-medicine/about/insights), [SciRev AIM](https://scirev.org/journal/artificial-intelligence-in-medicine/)). Skin Research and Technology has the best scope and the worst risk profile: a cluster of AI retractions, including a melanoma deep learning paper ([SRT retraction](https://doi.org/10.1111/srt.70296)). Journal of Imaging is ESCI-only, not SCIE, so the CHF 800 saving versus Diagnostics is not worth the indexing downgrade ([Journal of Imaging](https://www.mdpi.com/journal/jimaging)). None of these is predatory; the predatory risk is lookalike titles from OMICS International and Longdom Publishing ([Beall's List](https://beallslist.net/publishers), [FTC action](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/08/ftc-charges-academic-journal-publisher-omics-group-deceived-researchers)).

The readiness audit's suggestion to try Medical Image Analysis or CIBM first is the one part of its advice to discard. MIA's methods bar and 253-day timeline are wrong for this paper, CIBM's 12% acceptance rate makes it a long shot, and CMPB's 3,500-word cap makes it a rewrite rather than a submission. The audit was right about the statistical problems; it was wrong about the first target.

## The design is strong; the statistics are not yet defensible

The paper has a genuinely useful controlled design: **3 architectures x 3 conditions x 3 seeds**, a lesion-aware split, a validation-selected threshold at 95% specificity, public code, and per-sample predictions. That is better than most HAM10000 benchmark papers. The problem is the inference layer. The reported intervals, the AUC claim, the calibration mechanism, the external replication, and the XAI error-type comparison are all stronger than the tests behind them.

The interval problem is the most damaging because it is easy to audit. The aggregation code averages the per-run bootstrap bounds across seeds, and the ranking code takes the minimum lower bound, the maximum upper bound, and the maximum per-seed p-value ([collect_results.py](https://github.com/conorkz/melanoma-xai-bias/blob/e444228/scripts/collect_results.py), [analyze_ranking.py](https://github.com/conorkz/melanoma-xai-bias/blob/e444228/scripts/analyze_ranking.py)). The result is labeled a "95% CI" in the tables, but it is neither a per-run interval nor a seed-level interval for the mean. The direction of the ranking interaction is actually consistent: the ResNet-50 minus ViT-S/16 difference is **-0.328, -0.161, -0.108** across seeds, and the EfficientNet-B0 minus ViT-S/16 difference is **-0.177, -0.167, -0.140**; every per-seed 95% interval excludes zero (`results/ranking/ranking_tests.csv`). The summary envelope is not a proper pooled CI, but the sign is not in doubt. The fix is a hierarchical bootstrap or a seed-level t interval, plus per-seed values in the tables. It is a reporting repair, not a reversal of the finding.

The AUC claim is the missing analysis. The paper says "the AUC ranking is stable" in the abstract, Results, Discussion, and Conclusion, but there is no AUC difference test anywhere in the paper or the results directory. The point estimates are **0.914, 0.897, 0.854** at CE and **0.891, 0.884, 0.872** under focal for EfficientNet-B0, ResNet-50, and ViT-S/16. The order is preserved, but the ViT gains **+0.018** AUC while the CNNs lose **0.013 to 0.023**, and the EfficientNet-B0 versus ViT-S/16 gap narrows from **0.060 to 0.019**. A paired bootstrap over test images gives a focal difference of **-0.018 (95% CI -0.047 to +0.016)**, which is not distinguishable from zero. The honest claim is "the point-estimate ordering is preserved, but the ViT closes the gap; the sensitivity reversal is not purely a threshold effect." This is the single most important missing analysis in the paper.

The calibration claim is half right. Focal loss with class-balanced sampling worsens expected calibration error (ECE) by **4.4x** for ResNet-50 and **3.0x** for EfficientNet-B0, but only **1.36x** for ViT-S/16. "Roughly triples" is false for the ViT. More important, calibration does not explain the ViT's sensitivity gain because the ViT's AUC improves at the same time; the gain is a ranking improvement with a modest calibration change. The CNN loss is a mixture of ranking loss and calibration degradation. The ViT's CE ECE also has a standard deviation of **0.109**, so the "worst-calibrated CE baseline" ranking is not stable. The paper should say "calibration degradation tracks the CNN sensitivity loss; the ViT gain is not explained by calibration." That is a more interesting and more defensible mechanism story.

The external validation is suggestive, not confirmatory. The CNN means are negative: about **-7.7 points** for ResNet-50 and **-14.3 points** for EfficientNet-B0, with EfficientNet-B0 negative in every seed and ResNet-50 negative in two of three. The ViT changes are **+0.219, +0.128, -0.066**, a mean of **+0.094** that hides one negative seed and the largest seed SD. The CNN loss is the more consistent part of the external result; the ViT gain is the fragile part. HIBA also has no Fitzpatrick V-VI images and only 25 type IV images, none of them melanoma, so it tests domain shift, not dark-skin fairness. The paper should remove "reproduces the interaction" from the abstract, Results, and Conclusion, add per-seed SDs or CIs to Tables 10 and 11, and say "the external evidence is directionally consistent for the CNNs and suggestive for the ViT, not confirmatory." Recompute the 7.6 and 14.3 means from the raw CSV before reusing them.

The XAI analysis has a validity problem, not just a significance problem. `scripts/analyze_xai.py` sets the Grad-CAM target to `y_pred`, the predicted class ([analyze_xai.py](https://github.com/conorkz/melanoma-xai-bias/blob/e444228/scripts/analyze_xai.py)). For a false negative, the heatmap explains why the model predicted benign, not why it missed the melanoma. Comparing "mass inside the lesion" between false-negative and false-positive heatmaps is therefore confounded by the target class. The FN/FP mass difference is significant only for EfficientNet-B0 (**-0.229, 95% CI -0.363 to -0.086**); ResNet-50 is **-0.006 (-0.182 to +0.177)** and ViT-S/16 is **-0.080 (-0.187 to +0.032)**. The dark-versus-light attribution mass difference is significant for EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-S/16, but not ResNet-50. The fix is to rerun with the true class as the target, or to reframe the analysis as predicted-class attribution and remove the causal FN/FP interpretation. The XAI analysis also runs only on the CE-baseline models, so it does not test whether the focal intervention changes explanations; connect it to the intervention or present it as a separate descriptive analysis.

The fairness result is an error-type shift, not a statistically supported dark-skin AUC deficit. **0 of 54** dark-versus-light and dark-versus-medium AUC comparisons across the three cutoffs are significant; every bootstrap interval includes zero (`results/dark_skin/auc_differences.csv`). The false-positive L* odds ratio is significant only for ResNet-50 (**0.78, 0.61 to 0.94**); EfficientNet-B0 is **0.88 (0.67 to 1.11)** and ViT-S/16 is **0.77 (0.57 to 1.03)**. The false-negative L* odds ratio is significant for all three (**1.74, 1.66, 1.75**, all intervals exclude 1). The false-negative contrast odds ratio is significant only for ViT-S/16 (**0.48, 0.29 to 0.75**); the false-positive contrast odds ratios are all significant (**2.05, 1.69, 2.42**). Lead with the false-negative L* association; qualify the symmetric "darker skin more false positives, lighter skin more false negatives" story.

The verification pass clears one audit finding. Figure 3C is not a caption mismatch: the plotting code filters `outcome == "error"`, so the figure and the caption both describe the pooled error model. The audit's claim that the figure plots the false-positive model is wrong. Do not spend time "fixing" it.

## Repair in two days, submit in five, fall back to BMC

The fastest defensible path is a five-day sprint. Day 1 is the statistical rebuild: replace the averaged intervals and envelope p-values with a hierarchical bootstrap or seed-level t intervals, add paired AUC difference and interaction tests, add per-seed SDs to the external tables, run the strong-augmentation external evaluation, and run the matched-specificity analysis. Day 2 is the claim rewrite: cut the abstract to 250 words, rewrite the Introduction contributions, Discussion, and Conclusion to match the corrected results, and rewrite the cover letter. Day 3 is the JIIM build: convert to Word, blind the manuscript, separate the title page, anonymize the code link, and fix the tables and figures. Day 4 is the trace: verify every number against the CSVs, check the abstract and word counts, check the references, and run the reporting checklist. Day 5 is submission. If the temperature-scaling and true-class XAI runs are included, add two days.

The JIIM format is the main conversion cost. The journal asks for a **blinded manuscript** without author names or affiliations, a separate title page, **4 to 6 keywords**, an abstract of **150 to 250 words**, and Word rather than LaTeX ([JIIM guidelines](https://link.springer.com/journal/10278/submission-guidelines)). The current paper is a 25-page LaTeX build with 11 tables, 6 figures, 40 references, and a **323-word** abstract. Convert with Pandoc or the Springer Nature Word template, then fix the tables by hand; budget half a day. Number the references in citation order and use Index Medicus abbreviations. Supply figures as EPS or TIFF. The GitHub URL contains a personal username, which weakens blinding; use an anonymous repository or state that code will be released upon acceptance. Strengthen the existing data, code, ethics, consent, funding, and competing-interest statements; add CRediT contributions, the GenAI declaration, and the acknowledgments. The cover letter should state the audit contribution and the corrected claims, not the old "AUC stable" and "roughly triples" language. If the title stays as it is, the abstract and cover letter must define fairness as image-based lightness and error-type disparity, not Fitzpatrick V-VI clinical fairness; a title that names the dermoscopic setting and the error-type framing would preempt the objection.

The 250-word abstract should make four moves. Spend about 50 words on the question and design: three architectures, three conditions, three seeds, HAM10000, lesion-aware split, frozen threshold. Spend about 70 words on the main result: focal loss raises ViT sensitivity and lowers the CNNs; the CNN versus ViT interaction is negative in every seed; the within-architecture intervals include zero. Spend about 80 words on calibration and fairness: CNN ECE rises 3x to 4.4x, ViT 1.36x; the false-negative L* association is robust; the dark-versus-light AUC difference is not significant at any cutoff; the false-positive association is significant only for ResNet-50. Spend about 50 words on the external caveat: the CNN losses are broadly consistent, the ViT gain is seed-dependent, and the external evidence is suggestive, not confirmatory. Drop the XAI FN/FP numbers and the cutoff-specific sensitivity numbers; they are too detailed and they are the weakest claims.

The fallback is BMC Medical Imaging. It has no blinding requirement in its research-article guidelines, a **350-word** structured abstract with Background, Methods, Results, and Conclusions, **3 to 10 keywords**, and no strict manuscript word limit ([BMC research article](https://link.springer.com/journal/12880/submission-guidelines/research-article)). The current 323-word abstract fits the limit but must be restructured. The conversion from the JIIM version is two to three days: unblind, restructure the abstract, add the BMC Declarations block, and apply the BMC reference style. The cost is **$3,290**, and the median first decision is **22 days**. ## Fix the statistics before buying more data

The direct answer is: **fix the statistics first, then submit to JIIM. Do not submit now, and do not collect DDI or PASSION first.** The inference errors are not cosmetic. They are in the public code, and a statistically literate reviewer will find them because the repository is linked. A mid-tier venue is not a safe harbor: the same claims about AUC stability, calibration, external replication, and XAI error types will be checked at every tier. A major revision or rejection costs weeks, and the fallback to a gold open-access venue can cost an APC. Two days of repair with the existing predictions is the cheapest way to protect the paper.

The repair also makes the paper stronger. The core findings survive: the CNN external means are negative, the EfficientNet-B0 versus ViT interaction is negative in every seed, and the false-negative L* association is significant for all three architectures. The corrected paper tells a more credible story: focal loss helps the ViT and hurts the CNNs, the CNN loss is partly calibration and partly ranking, the ViT gain is a ranking improvement that does not replicate in every external seed, and the fairness signal is an error-type shift rather than a dark-skin AUC deficit. That is a better paper than the current overclaimed version, not a weaker one.

DDI and PASSION would not fix the inference. They are clinical-image datasets, while HAM10000 and HIBA are dermoscopic; a cross-modality comparison would confound skin tone with domain shift, which the paper already acknowledges. They would not produce a clean Fitzpatrick V-VI dermoscopic estimate, and Pipsqueak's **16 core images** are too few for a stable sensitivity estimate. Collecting or curating them would take weeks to months, and the paper would still need the statistical repair. They are a follow-up study, not a precondition for this submission.

The exception is strategic, not statistical. If the goal is a fairness-focused clinical dermatology journal such as JAMA Dermatology, the British Journal of Dermatology, or JEADV, the lack of Fitzpatrick V-VI data is a likely rejection reason, and no amount of statistical repair will fix it. In that case the paper needs a different framing or a new data collection. But that is not the recommended path. For JIIM and BMC Medical Imaging, the FST V-VI gap is a limitation to state clearly, not a fatal flaw.

## Conclusion

The gap that matters is not between the paper and a journal; it is between the design and the inference. The design is stronger than most HAM10000 benchmarks, and the core findings survive a repair that costs two days and no retraining. The current claims do not: the intervals are not intervals, the AUC ranking is untested, the external ViT gain is seed-dependent, and the Grad-CAM analysis explains the wrong class. Fixing those claims makes the paper more honest and more interesting, because the real story is architecture-dependent: the CNNs lose sensitivity and calibration, the ViT gains ranking quality, and the fairness signal is an error-type shift.

The fastest defensible path is JIIM under the subscription route, with BMC Medical Imaging as the paid fallback and Diagnostics as the budget branch. The slowest path is to collect more data before fixing the statistics. The paper does not need more data to be publishable; it needs its uncertainty reported correctly and its claims cut to what the data support.
